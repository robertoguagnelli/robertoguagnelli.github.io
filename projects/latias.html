MY CONTRIBUTION

Design ownership. Engineering integration.

As Design Captain & Engineering Coordinator, I made the aircraft design decisions with research support from a five-person design team. I coordinated more than 40 engineering members across design, structures, electronics and R&D to integrate the aircraft.

Concept to detailed CAD

I developed the conceptual and theoretical design and personally modeled more than 300 parts within an assembly of over 400 components, including simplified, clean geometry for analysis.

Analysis and trade studies

I evaluated mass, center of gravity, materials, geometry, stability, control, propulsion and cargo-bay concepts. My work included CFD, motor and propeller selection, and impact-test design.

01 / AIRCRAFT INTEGRATION

Moving to twin-engine bottle transport.

Latias introduced the team’s first twin-engine configuration, bottle payloads in place of ballast, and a smaller battery. I coordinated the propulsion, cargo, aerodynamic and structural interfaces. The aircraft demonstrated 6 kg of payload, compared with Wingull’s 4 kg.

A flexible PETG bottle retainer

I developed a printed retainer inspired by the shape of a LEGO hand, using the elastic flexibility of PETG to hold the bottle and allow repeated loading.

First iteration

Concentric inner and outer radii created a constant-thickness curved section. Insertion and removal required excessive force, and the part occasionally fractured.

Revised geometry

I offset the outer radius to create a thicker base and thinner tips. This made insertion easier while resisting extraction. Added tabs let the operator open the retainer by hand for removal while preserving bottle retention.

The change in operating force was observed during handling; no force measurements are claimed.

02 / TEST AND ITERATE

Validating retention and impact resistance.

50 insertion / removal cycles

The final retainer completed 50 cycles, prolonged static loading and impact tests without observed cracks, permanent deformation or accidental bottle release under the tested conditions.

Fuselage and landing gear

The documented drop test used a 12 kg test configuration and an 11.04 cm drop height. No fractures, cracks or permanent deformation were observed in the fuselage or landing gear.

The 12 kg impact-test configuration is separate from the later demonstrated 13 kg total flight mass. The static retention check does not establish long-term creep life.

ANALYSIS TO OPERATION

Predicting takeoff under local conditions.

I used drag coefficients from CFD in the final takeoff-run model to estimate allowable payload for the density altitude of the day. Aerodynamic work combined theory, XFLR5 and CFD.

Flight testing took place at Atizapán, where air density could fall to approximately 0.7 kg/m³. An uneven runway and terrain-related wind and turbulence made testing more demanding than the Fort Worth design conditions.

What we verified

We adjusted the reference takeoff-distance limit to local conditions and checked that the aircraft became airborne before it. We did not collect measured takeoff distances for a quantitative prediction-error comparison.

RESULTS & REFLECTION

From engineering decisions to flight.

Latias flew with 6 kg of payload at an empty mass of 7 kg, for 13 kg total. This represented 50% more demonstrated payload than Wingull, under different aircraft configurations and operating conditions. Flight experience also indicated improved stability.
